A lot happens on a road site in a single day: a lorry of ballast arrives, it rains for two hours, a pipe turns up that is not on the drawings, one section is compacted and a drainage channel is cast. That evening, everyone remembers. Three months later, when the progress statements are checked or delays are discussed, nobody remembers exactly. That is when the **daily site diary** matters.

## What a daily site diary is

It is a short record, filled in every working day, of what was built, with what resources and under what conditions. It can be a notebook, a printed form or a digital document. The format matters less than the habit: one entry per day, written on the same day, by the same person or team.

The diary does not replace the formal documents of the works, such as inspection reports or the technical file. For those, check the requirements of the current Romanian norms and your contract. But the diary is the source that makes those documents easy to complete correctly.

## What to record every day

A good entry answers a few simple questions:

- **Where work took place**: the section, chainage or street, as precisely as possible.
- **What was done**: for example excavation, sub-base, drainage channels or culverts, with approximate quantities.
- **Who and with what**: the size of the crew, the machines on site and the ones standing idle.
- **Materials delivered**: what arrived, how much and from whom, with a reference to the delivery notes.
- **Weather**: rain, frost or heat explain many stoppages and some quality problems.
- **Problems and stoppages**: unexpected utilities, missing materials, waiting for an instruction from the designer or the client.
- **Inspections**: what was checked, by whom, and whether a report was signed.
- **Instructions received**: who asked for a change, when and how.

Add photos with the date and location. A picture of a drain before it is covered, or of a pipe found in a trench, is worth far more than a description from memory.

## Why it is worth the effort

### Progress statements are easier to support

A progress statement is the document used to claim payment for the quantities built in a given period, and those quantities must be justified. If the diary shows day by day what was done and where, checking goes quickly and there are fewer disputes. Volume surveys then confirm the final figures.

### Delays show up early

A works schedule shows what should happen. The diary shows what actually happened. Compared once a week, the two reveal straight away whether an activity is falling behind and why. Moving a crew or ordering materials in time is much easier than recovering a lost month.

### Disputes have a clear starting point

If work stopped for three days because of an unexpected utility, the diary shows when it was found, who was notified and when work resumed. Without such a note, the discussion becomes a matter of memory and one person's word against another's.

### Hidden works stay documented

Sub-base layers, drains and pipes end up under the asphalt. After that, the only evidence of how they were built is the notes, photos and inspection reports. The diary ties them all to a date and a place.

## Common mistakes

- **Filling it in at the end of the week.** Details get lost, and notes written from memory carry less weight.
- **Entries that are too vague.** "Worked on the road" helps nobody. Location and quantity make the difference.
- **Only good news.** Stoppages and problems are exactly what will be discussed later.
- **A separate diary for every crew**, with nobody bringing them together. Someone needs the full picture.
