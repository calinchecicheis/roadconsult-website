On paper, a road is a clear line: centreline, widths, slopes, levels. On site, at the start, there is only a field, an old track or a street lined with houses. Between the two sits **setting out**: the work of moving the design onto the ground, point by point, so the excavator, grader and paver work exactly where and as deep as they should.

## What setting out means in practice

The surveyor takes the coordinates from the design and marks them physically on site, usually with pegs, nails or spray paint. For a road, the main items are:

- **the centreline**, with the start and end points of each curve;
- **the edges** of the carriageway, shoulders and ditches;
- **the levels**, the height each layer must reach so water drains the right way;
- **small structures** such as culverts and gutters, which must tie in properly with the road.

Setting out is not a one-off job. As the works move from earthworks to sub-base and then to asphalt, the points are re-marked and checked.

## Which instruments are used

At Road Consult we use three types of Leica equipment, each with its own role:

- **The total station** (Leica FlexLine TS09plus) measures angles and distances from known points. It is the core instrument for precise setting out.
- **The GNSS receiver** (Leica Viva GS08plus) positions itself by satellite. It is fast on large, open areas without obstructions.
- **The digital level** (Leica Sprinter 250M) checks levels. It confirms that the slopes in the design are actually there on the ground.

The choice depends on the site. On a narrow street with buildings and trees, the total station is usually the safer option. On a long farm road, GNSS saves a lot of time.

## The survey comes first

Before setting out, you usually need a **topographic survey**: a measurement of the ground as it is today. It produces the site plan, the long and cross sections and, importantly, the **volume calculation** for cut and fill.

For the client, volumes go straight into the budget. The gap between estimated and actual volume can mean extra lorries of material or, the other way round, paid work that was never needed.

## Which mistakes correct setting out prevents

Problems caused by poor setting out show up late, when they are expensive to fix:

- **Standing water on the road.** A reversed or too shallow crossfall lets water pool, and the road wears out faster.
- **A road that doesn't meet what is already there.** Tie-ins to gates, junctions and culverts must come out at the right level, or you get steps and dips.
- **Uneven widths.** The eye immediately notices a road that narrows for no reason.
- **Disputes at handover.** If the final measurements don't match the design, the works can be challenged.

## What the client can ask for

If you are commissioning road works, it is worth asking the contractor or consultant for:

1. the initial topographic survey and the volumes calculated from it;
2. the reference points used, so every later measurement refers to the same base;
3. level checks on each layer, not only at the end;
4. a final as-built survey to compare with the design.

## In short

Setting out is the moment the design becomes real. Done properly and checked along the way, it saves money, time and arguments. Done in a hurry, it only shows once the asphalt has cooled.
